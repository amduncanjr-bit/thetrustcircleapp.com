What to do in the minutes after a car crash — and how crash detection changes them

The moments right after a collision are disorienting, so knowing what to do before you're in one makes a real difference.

A crash scrambles everything at once. Your adrenaline spikes, your thinking narrows, and the sequence of what actually needs to happen can disappear completely. Running through it now, while you're calm, means some of that sequence will surface when you need it.

The first sixty seconds

Before you move, take a breath and do a quick body check. Pain can be masked by shock, so move slowly and deliberately. If you or anyone else is seriously injured — unconscious, bleeding heavily, not breathing normally — call 911 immediately and don't wait for anything else. That call is always the first priority.

If you can move safely, check on passengers. Don't move anyone who complains of neck or back pain unless there's an immediate danger like fire. Turn on your hazard lights. If the cars are drivable and you're on a busy road, move them to the shoulder to reduce secondary collision risk — but only if it's genuinely safe and your local laws allow it. Know your local laws here; they vary.

Once you've handled the immediate safety picture, document everything you can. Photos of both vehicles, the positions they came to rest in, any visible damage, the road conditions, traffic signs nearby. Do this before anything gets moved. Your memory will be confident later and probably wrong; the photos won't be.

Exchanging information without the chaos

You need the other driver's name, license plate, driver's license number, insurance company, and policy number. They need the same from you. Witnesses are valuable — if someone stops, ask for their name and number before they leave.

Stay factual and stay calm. What you say at the scene can matter later, so describe what happened without assigning fault.

Notify your insurance company as soon as you reasonably can. Most policies require prompt reporting regardless of who was at fault. If there's any dispute about injuries, fault, or property damage later, talk to a lawyer rather than guessing at your rights.

One practical thing people often forget: note the time, date, and exact location. A cross street or highway marker is enough. If the police respond, get the report number before they leave.

Where crash detection fits into this

Crash detection has changed one specific part of the post-crash sequence: the moment when you might not be able to do anything at all. If you're unconscious, disoriented, or trapped, you may not be able to call for help yourself. That's precisely where an automatic detection system can bridge the gap.

When Trust Circle's crash detection triggers, it starts a countdown you can cancel if you're fine — a glancing pothole won't accidentally summon your contacts. If you don't cancel, it can automatically call 911, and your chosen people are alerted so they know something happened and where you are. The screen shows you as each alert moves from sent to delivered to opened, and the first person to respond becomes the lead contact. If there's no connection, the app tells you rather than pretending the alert went out.

That matters most in the scenarios where the old sequence breaks down entirely: a rural road, a late hour, a crash bad enough that you genuinely can't reach your phone. Detection doesn't replace the steps above — you'll still need to document the scene, exchange information, and call your insurer. But it compresses the window between the crash and someone knowing about it, which in serious cases is the window that counts most.

The best time to set this up is before you need it. Check that your emergency contacts are current, that the people listed are ones who'd actually respond, and that you understand what the countdown looks and feels like. A few minutes now means the system works the way you expect if it ever actually fires.
