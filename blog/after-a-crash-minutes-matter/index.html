What to do in the first minutes after a car crash

The moments right after a collision are disorienting, but a simple mental checklist can keep you safer until help arrives.

A crash happens fast. One second you're driving; the next, everything is still and strange. Your brain is flooded with adrenaline, which is useful for moving you out of danger but terrible for clear thinking. That's exactly why it helps to have a rough plan before you ever need it.

Check yourself, then check on others

Before you get out of the car, take three seconds to assess yourself. Are you bleeding? Is there pain that might mean you shouldn't move? If the vehicle is in a safe position — not on fire, not in active traffic — staying put briefly to do that check can prevent a second injury.

Once you know you can move safely, check on any passengers. Then check on people in the other vehicle. Do not move anyone who is unconscious or complaining of neck or back pain unless there is an immediate danger like fire or rising water. Moving someone with a spinal injury can make it far worse.

Call emergency services. Even if the crash looks minor, call. Injuries are not always visible right away — adrenaline masks pain — and a dispatcher can help you figure out whether an ambulance is needed. While you wait, turn on your hazard lights and, if you have them, set out road flares or warning triangles to alert other drivers.

If it's safe to do so, move vehicles out of the lane of traffic. A stationary car in a live lane causes secondary crashes. Pull to a shoulder or a nearby parking lot, then stop and don't drive further until you've documented everything.

Document the scene before memories fade

Your memory of the crash will start changing within minutes — that's just how trauma and adrenaline work. Take photos immediately: damage to all vehicles, license plates, the position of cars before they're moved, road conditions, any skid marks, traffic signals, and anything else that might matter later. Get the other driver's name, license number, insurance information, and contact details. Get names and phone numbers from any witnesses before they leave.

Write down your own account of what happened as soon as you can — even a voice memo to yourself counts — because the details you capture in the first hour are the ones you'll trust most later.

Notify your insurance company promptly. Know your local laws about what must be reported to police — requirements vary significantly by location and by the severity of damage or injury. If you're uncertain about your rights or obligations, talk to a lawyer before giving recorded statements to anyone.

One thing people often forget: notify someone who cares about you. Shock can set in slowly, and driving yourself home afterward — even after what feels like a fender-bender — may not be safe. Having a person on the way to you matters.

Where crash detection fits into all of this

The hard truth about severe crashes is that the injured person may not be able to do any of the above. They may be unconscious, disoriented, or trapped. That's where automatic crash detection changes things.

Trust Circle's crash detection monitors for the kind of sudden force associated with a collision. If it triggers and you don't respond, your circle — the people you've chosen and who have consented to be there — gets an alert with your live location. They can see where you are and respond immediately, rather than waiting and wondering.

This doesn't replace calling 911. Emergency services should always be the first call in a serious crash. But your circle knowing what happened and where you are — simultaneously, without you having to do anything — means the people who love you aren't left in the dark while you wait for help to arrive.

The steps here are worth rehearsing mentally before you need them. A crash is already enough to manage. Knowing roughly what comes next means one less thing your stunned brain has to figure out from scratch.
