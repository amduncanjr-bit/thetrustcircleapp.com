What to do in the minutes after a car crash

Those first few minutes are disorienting by design — here is how to move through them without making things worse.

A crash scrambles you. Even a minor one. Your heart rate spikes, your hands shake, and your brain — which was just on autopilot — is suddenly asked to make a dozen decisions at once. Knowing roughly what to do before it happens is one of the few things that actually cuts through that fog.

The first sixty seconds

Before anything else: stop the car if it's still moving, and check whether you're hurt. Sometimes adrenaline masks pain, so do a slow, deliberate inventory — head, neck, chest, limbs. If something feels wrong, stay as still as you can and wait for help rather than climbing out and potentially making an injury worse.

If you can move safely, turn on your hazard lights. At night, or on a fast road, staying inside with your seatbelt on is often safer than standing near traffic. If there is a fire, smoke, or fuel smell, get everyone out and move well away from the vehicle.

Call 911 — or your local emergency number — even if the crash looks minor. You may not be able to assess the other driver's condition from your car. A police report also matters for insurance purposes, and an ambulance can evaluate injuries you might be underestimating.

Adrenaline is unreliable. What feels like nothing right now may need attention in an hour — always let a medical professional make that call.

Once you know everyone is safe

If it is safe to do so, document everything before anything moves. Photos of both vehicles, the positions they came to rest in, any visible damage, skid marks, the surrounding road — all of it. Take more than you think you need. Write down (or voice-memo) what you remember about the sequence of events while it's fresh, because memory shifts quickly after a stressful event.

Exchange information with the other driver: name, contact number, insurance company, policy number, and license plate. Be factual and civil. Know your local laws around what you're required to share; if you have questions about liability or what to say, talk to a lawyer before making statements beyond the basics.

Notify someone you trust that you've been in a crash — even a small one. It matters that someone who cares about you knows where you are, what happened, and that you're okay. That call or text can wait until things are stable, but don't skip it entirely.

How crash detection fits in

Most of the time, you will be conscious and able to do all of the above yourself. But crashes don't always leave you that option. Crash detection exists for the gap between a serious impact and the moment someone realizes something is wrong.

When Trust Circle's crash detection triggers, it starts a countdown you can cancel if you're fine — a simple way to handle the false positives that come with any motion-based system. If you don't cancel, it can place an automatic call to 911. That setting is yours to turn on or leave off; the app is straightforward about what it will and won't do.

Your chosen people are also notified, so someone in your circle knows something happened and where you are, without you having to find your phone and think clearly enough to dial. That matters most in the scenarios where you can't do those things.

It doesn't replace any of the steps above. It doesn't file the police report or take the photos or exchange insurance information. What it does is compress the time between impact and someone knowing — and in a serious crash, that window is the one that counts.

Set it up on a calm Tuesday. That way, if you ever need it, it's already running.
