The campus safety conversation every parent and student should have before move-in

A few honest conversations now will matter more than any piece of gear you pack.

Move-in weekend is loud and warm and full of logistics. You're assembling furniture, arguing about where the lamp goes, trying to memorize the parking rules. A real safety conversation tends to get bumped. Then the car pulls away and both sides assume the other one has a plan.

They usually don't. Not a shared one, anyway.

This isn't about fear. It's about making a few decisions together, while you're still in the same room, so neither of you has to improvise later under stress. Here's what that conversation actually needs to cover.

Build a simple check-in rhythm — and agree on what silence means

The most useful thing a student and their family can agree on isn't an app or a device. It's a shared understanding of normal. What does regular contact look like for your family? A Sunday call? A quick text after late nights out? There's no universal right answer. The point is that both sides agree in advance, so an unusual silence means something instead of nothing.

Once you have a rhythm, decide what happens when it breaks. Who tries to reach you first? How long do they wait? Who's the backup contact if the first person can't get through? Write this down. A plan that only exists in someone's head doesn't transfer well under pressure.

Students: this isn't your parents tracking your every move. It's a simple signal-and-response system that protects you without requiring you to narrate your life. You're defining the terms together, not having them imposed.

Know the campus resources before you need them

Most students know their campus has a safety office and an emergency line. Far fewer know the number off the top of their head, or where the blue-light emergency stations are on their specific route home, or whether the campus escort service runs on weekends.

Spend twenty minutes on the campus safety website before move-in day. Find the non-emergency line, the after-hours mental health line, and the safe-ride or escort option if one exists. Screenshot them. Put the non-emergency number in your phone under a name you'll actually recognize in a hurry.

Knowing a resource exists is different from knowing how to use it at midnight when you're stressed. That gap is worth closing now.

Parents can help here by looking this up together with their student rather than just emailing a list of links. Walking through it as a team means the student is more likely to actually remember it.

Have the harder conversations too

Campus safety isn't only about walking home alone. It includes knowing what to do if you're in a situation where someone is hurt or in crisis, what your options are if you feel unsafe with someone you know, and what your rights are if you're ever stopped by campus security or local police. Know your local laws and, where those situations involve legal questions, talk to a lawyer or a campus advocate — not just a parent guessing from a distance.

Students: tell at least two people in your life — one on campus, one off — enough about your routines that they'd notice if something was wrong. Not because bad things are likely, but because that small redundancy costs you almost nothing and matters a great deal in the rare moment it's needed.

Parents: the goal of this conversation isn't to make your student feel watched. It's to hand them tools and then trust them to use those tools. They're more likely to reach out when something goes wrong if they don't expect the call to trigger a panic.

You don't need to solve every scenario. You need to know each other's defaults — how you'll stay in touch, who you'll call first, and where to find help on campus. That much, you can settle before the car is even unloaded.
