The campus safety conversation every parent and student should have before move-in

One conversation before the first goodbye can make the whole year feel a little less like a leap into the unknown.

Move-in day is loud. There are boxes and bedding and awkward hallway introductions and somebody always forgets a power strip. It is not, on the surface, a great time to talk about safety. But that conversation — the real one, not the quick hug-and-go version — is worth carving out time for, because the first few weeks of college are when new students are most likely to be navigating unfamiliar places, unfamiliar people, and unfamiliar judgment calls, all at once.

This is not about fear. It is about setting each other up so that nobody is left guessing when something actually happens.

Start with the practical stuff most people skip

Before you talk about feelings or rules or check-in schedules, walk through the concrete facts together. Where is the campus emergency line, and is it saved in the student's phone? Where are the blue-light emergency stations, and does the student know their campus map well enough to actually find one at night? What does the student's campus escort or late-night shuttle service cover, and what are the hours?

Talk about the dorm itself. Who has a key? Who should have a key? What is the protocol if someone is locked out late? Does the building have a front-desk check-in after hours, and does it actually get enforced? These sound like logistical questions, but they are safety questions wearing ordinary clothes.

Go over the student's health information together — not to micromanage, but to make sure a roommate or close friend knows about any allergies, conditions, or medications that might matter in an emergency. That person does not need a full medical history. They just need to know enough to help.

Agree on what staying in touch actually looks like

This is where a lot of families talk past each other. Parents often want frequent contact as reassurance. Students often feel surveilled. The fix is to make the agreement specific and mutual before anyone feels crowded.

A check-in system only works if both sides actually agree to it — and if everyone understands that silence is the thing that triggers concern, not absence.

Decide together: how often, which app or method, and what happens if a check-in is missed. Is a missed check-in a text, a call, or something more? There is no single right answer. What matters is that both sides know the plan and have agreed to it, rather than one side assuming and the other feeling watched.

Also talk about what the student should do — not just what the parent will do — if something feels wrong. Who is their first call? Is it a parent, a roommate, campus security, a local non-emergency line? Running through those scenarios out loud before they happen makes them much easier to act on under stress.

Loop in the student's circle, not just the family

Parents are essential, but they are usually hours away. The people who can actually show up are on campus. Encourage your student to identify two or three people — a roommate, a hallmate, a friend from orientation — who know their general schedule and would notice if something seemed off.

This does not require an elaborate system. It requires the student to have had a low-stakes version of this same conversation with someone nearby: hey, if you ever can't reach me and something seems wrong, here's what to do. That is the whole thing. It sounds small, and it is genuinely useful.

If your student wants a more structured way to share their location on a specific night — a late walk home, a trip to an off-campus party, a rideshare alone — apps like Trust Circle let them share a live trip with specific people for that trip only, without giving anyone permanent access. Consent-based and revocable. That kind of tool works best when the student has already chosen who their circle is and why, which is exactly the conversation worth having before move-in day.

You will probably have this talk in a parking lot with a half-assembled bed frame in the background. That is fine. Have it anyway.
