The campus safety conversation every parent and student should have before move-in

A honest, practical talk before the first goodbye can make all the difference when something goes wrong far from home.

Move-in day is loud and fast. You're hauling boxes, meeting roommates, figuring out where the dining hall is. The safety conversation often gets squeezed out entirely, or reduced to a quick "be careful" on the way out the door. That's worth fixing — not because college is uniquely dangerous, but because it's the first time most students are managing their own safety without a parent nearby to notice something feels off.

The goal isn't to hand over a rulebook. It's to think through a few real scenarios together, so neither of you is improvising alone when one actually happens.

Start with the practical basics, not the scary stuff

Before anything else, make sure you both know the campus's emergency number. Most universities have a dedicated line separate from 911, and it often reaches officers who know the campus layout. Save it in the student's phone as a contact, not just a note somewhere. Do the same with the number for campus health services and the after-hours crisis line, which most schools offer.

Talk about walking routes. Which paths are well-lit at night? Where are the emergency blue-light stations on campus? Does the school offer a safe-ride or escort service for late nights? A lot of students don't find out these resources exist until they need them, which is the worst moment to be searching.

Discuss the dorm itself. Does the building require a key card? What's the protocol if a door is propped open? It sounds small, but building security habits early matters more than any single rule. The student who automatically lets the door close behind them is doing more than the one who knows all the policies but never acts on them.

Knowing one trusted person expects to hear from you — and will actually follow up — is one of the most underrated safety tools there is.

Set up a simple check-in rhythm. It doesn't have to be daily, and it definitely shouldn't feel like surveillance. Maybe it's a Sunday text, or a standing phone call every few days. The point is that someone at home has a baseline sense of how things are going, so that a missed check-in registers as unusual rather than normal.

Have the harder conversations before you need them

This is where a lot of families stop short. Talking about alcohol, substances, and social pressure is uncomfortable, but it's worth doing plainly. Not as a lecture — as a genuine conversation about judgment and options. What does the student do if they're at a party and feel unsafe? Do they have a friend they can text a code word to? Do they know they can call home without judgment at 2 a.m.?

Talk about what to do if they witness something that seems wrong. Bystander intervention programs exist on many campuses and are worth looking into before the semester starts. Knowing that stepping in doesn't have to mean physical confrontation — sometimes it's just creating a distraction or getting a friend out of a situation — makes it easier to actually do something.

If the student has a car, go over what to do during a traffic stop: hands visible, speak calmly, know where documents are. Know your local laws about recording interactions. The real work, though, is the conversation itself. Have it before the boxes are unpacked, when you still have each other's full attention.
