Rideshare safety: the two minutes between the curb and the car

The moments before you get in are when most rideshare problems start, and a few habits can change them completely.

You ordered the car, you're outside, and your phone says it's two minutes away. Those two minutes are where attention usually drops. You're half-watching the map, half-scrolling something else, and when a car pulls up you move toward it almost automatically. That reflex is worth slowing down.

Before you get in, verify everything

Every rideshare platform gives you the same three things to check: the license plate, the make and model of the car, and the driver's name. Check all three, in that order, before you open the door. The plate is the hardest thing to fake, so start there. Look at it before the driver can say anything to you — don't let a friendly wave or a question skip you past that step.

Then ask the driver who they're picking up. Let them say your name. You saying it first defeats the purpose. If the plate matches and the driver says your name unprompted, you have two good signals. If either one is off, step back and contact the platform. A real driver won't be offended. Someone who gets aggressive when you verify is telling you something important.

At night or somewhere unfamiliar, do this check under actual light. Move toward a streetlamp if you need to. Taking an extra fifteen seconds to read a plate clearly is not awkward. It's just sense.

Your position on the curb matters more than you think

While you're waiting, stand somewhere with your back toward a wall or a building face rather than the street. This keeps you from being startled from behind and lets you watch traffic approaching from both directions. If you're waiting at night, stay near other people when you can — a lit doorway, a group of people nearby. You don't need to stand alone at the edge of the curb staring at the map for ninety seconds. Step back. Stay visible to cameras and passersby, but put something solid behind you.

Keep the phone you're tracking the car on in your hand or jacket pocket, not buried in a bag. You want both hands reasonably free when the car arrives. If you're wearing headphones, take at least one out while you wait. You want to hear what's coming.

Tell someone before you get in, not after

This is the habit most people skip because it feels like extra work when you're tired or in a hurry. But sharing your trip before you get in — not five minutes into the ride — means someone already knows your driver, your route, and your destination if anything goes wrong in between.

A quick text works. A screenshot of the driver info works. The important thing is that it happens before the door closes. If you use an app like Trust Circle, you can set up a trip beforehand so a chosen person gets notified automatically when your estimated arrival time passes — without you having to remember to check in mid-ride. A guest link means they can follow that one trip even if they don't have the app, and it expires when your trip ends.

None of this is about expecting something to go wrong. Most rides are fine. But the two minutes before you get in are the only time you have full control over who sees what and what you've verified. Once the door closes, your options narrow. So do your checking, your positioning, and your sharing while you're still standing on the curb — when all of those things are still easy.
