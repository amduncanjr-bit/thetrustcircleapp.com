Rideshare safety: the two minutes between the curb and the car

The moments before you get in are when most of the risk actually lives.

You booked the ride, you watched the dot move across the map, and now you're standing outside waiting. It feels like the safe part. You're not in the car yet, nothing has happened yet. But those two minutes at the curb are worth paying close attention to, because they're when your decisions matter most.

Verify before you open the door

The standard advice exists for good reason: check the license plate, check the car make and color, check the driver's face against the photo in the app. Do all three, in that order, every time. Not just when it's late, not just when you're alone. Every time makes it habit; habit means you do it when you're tired or distracted or in a hurry.

Then ask the driver to confirm your name before you say it. A simple "who are you here for?" is all it takes. A legitimate driver will have it. This one small reversal puts the confirmation burden where it belongs.

Don't get in if anything is off. A mismatched plate, a driver who seems unsure of your name, a car that doesn't match — those are reasons to step back, end the ride in the app, and request another. You don't owe anyone an explanation. The app makes canceling easy for a reason.

Ask the driver who they're picking up before you say your name. It's a small habit that puts the verification where it belongs.

What you do with your body and your phone while you wait

Stay aware of the space around you while you're at the curb. That doesn't mean being paranoid — it means not being absorbed in your screen to the point where you wouldn't notice a car pulling up fast or someone approaching from behind. Glance up. Know roughly who else is nearby.

Pick a waiting spot that's visible and lit. If you're inside a bar or restaurant, you can often watch for the car from just inside the door and walk out when it arrives, rather than standing alone on the sidewalk for several minutes. Many rideshare apps let you text the driver — use that to coordinate rather than standing outside longer than you need to.

Share your trip details before you get in, not after. Most rideshare apps have a share-trip feature that sends a link with the driver's info and your route. Send it. If the person you're sending it to doesn't have the app, a text with the driver's name, plate, and where you're going does the same job. It takes twenty seconds and it means someone knows where you are and who you're with.

If you use Trust Circle, the Walk Home or Safe Trip feature lets you set a time estimate and choose who watches. The lock screen shows the trip's status so you don't have to keep the app open. If the estimate passes without you marking it done, your watchers are notified. That's useful for any kind of trip, rideshare included — start it before you get in the car, end it when you're through the door at the other end.

Once you're inside

Sit in the back seat. It gives you more options and more space. Note where the door handles are. Keep your phone charged enough to make a call. If the route looks wrong, it's fine to say so: "I think this route is off" is a normal thing to say. You can also open the navigation in your own app and follow along.

If something feels seriously wrong — route that makes no sense, driver who won't respond to you, door that won't open — call 911. That's always the right move. No app replaces it.

Most rideshare trips are uneventful. The habits above are there for the ones that aren't, and they cost almost nothing to build.
