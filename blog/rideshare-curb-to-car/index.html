Rideshare safety: the two minutes between the curb and the car

The moments before you get in are when most rideshare incidents begin, and a few deliberate habits can change that.

You open the app, the car is two minutes away, and you step outside to wait. For most people, that's where active thinking about safety stops. But those two minutes — and the thirty seconds after the door closes — are worth paying attention to. Not because something bad is likely to happen, but because the habits that protect you are quick and easy to build before you ever need them.

Before you get in

The single most important thing you can do is verify the vehicle before you touch the handle. That means checking the license plate against what the app shows you — not the other way around. A driver who calls out your name first is not automatically your driver. Anyone can learn a name. You confirm the plate, then the make and color, then you ask the driver to tell you your name. All three. It takes fifteen seconds.

While you're waiting, resist the pull to stand at the curb scrolling your phone with earbuds in. You don't need to look paranoid or aggressive. You just need to be present enough to notice if the car that pulls up doesn't match. Stand near the entrance of the building if you can, or a spot with decent lighting, rather than walking to the end of the block to meet the car in a darker stretch. Small adjustments, real difference.

If a car pulls up and the driver seems impatient about the verification questions, that's information. You can cancel and request a new ride. You don't owe an explanation.

Verifying a driver takes fifteen seconds. It's the only moment in the whole trip where you can walk away easily.

Once the door closes

Notice the child locks. On many vehicles, rear doors can be locked from the driver's controls so they won't open from the inside. You can't tell just by looking. A quick mental note of whether your door opens normally at the start of a ride is a habit worth developing.

Sit behind the passenger seat when you're alone. It gives you a cleaner exit from either rear door and keeps more distance between you and the driver. Share your trip status through whatever method your app offers — most rideshare platforms have a built-in share feature. Use it. Let at least one person know you're in the car, who the driver is, and where you're headed. A text takes ten seconds.

If something feels off during the ride — a route that doesn't match, a driver who is aggressively asking personal questions, a lock you can't operate — trust that feeling. You can fake a phone call. You can ask to be dropped at a public place rather than your exact address. You can text someone your location. None of these things require you to escalate or confront anyone.

Making this a routine, not a reaction

Safety habits work because they're automatic. If you only run through the verification checklist when you're already nervous, you're working harder in the moment you can least afford to. Run it every time — boring trips included — until it's just part of how you get in a car.

If you want a layer of backup, Trust Circle's Safe Trip feature shares your journey live with people you choose and alerts them if your trip stalls or you don't arrive. Guardian mode lets one trusted person watch your route in real time until you're home. Both are built for exactly this kind of situation — a routine trip where you'd like someone quietly paying attention.

But the core habits don't require an app or a plan or anything beyond a few seconds of attention before you open the door. Plate, make, name. Sit, share, notice. That's the whole thing. Do it every time and it costs you almost nothing. The one time it matters, you'll be glad it was already automatic.
