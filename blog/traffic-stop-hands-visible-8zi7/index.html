Traffic stops: keeping your hands visible while your documents do the talking

A calm, prepared driver makes a traffic stop safer for everyone in the car.

Lights in the mirror. That particular sinking feeling. Whether you were speeding or have no idea why you're being pulled over, the next few minutes matter — and most of them happen before the officer reaches your window. What you do in that gap sets the tone for everything that follows.

Pull over as soon as it's safe, signal as you do it, and choose a well-lit spot if you have any choice. Turn off the engine. If it's dark, turn on the interior light. These aren't just courtesies — they lower the temperature of the whole encounter before a word is exchanged.

Hands first, everything else second

Keep your hands where they can be seen. On the steering wheel is the clearest place. Don't reach for your glove compartment, your bag, or your phone until you've said out loud what you're about to do and the officer has acknowledged it. Something simple works fine: "My registration is in the glove box — okay if I get it?" Then wait for a yes before you move.

This feels slow. It is slow. That's the point. Sudden movements in a tense situation create risk, and you can remove most of that risk just by narrating your actions before you take them.

Know where your documents are before you need them. License, registration, proof of insurance — if you have to dig through a pile of receipts and old takeout napkins while an officer waits, you're adding friction neither of you wants. A small folder or envelope in the glove box takes thirty seconds to put together and pays off every time.

The calmer and clearer you are, the more control you actually have over how the stop goes.

Speak clearly and answer questions directly. You're not required to narrate your entire day, but being curt doesn't help you. If you're unsure about your rights in a specific situation — whether you're required to answer a particular question, for example — that's genuinely worth knowing before you need it. Know your local laws, and if something happens during a stop that concerns you, talk to a lawyer afterward rather than trying to resolve it at the roadside.

Passengers, phones, and staying calm under pressure

If you have passengers, let them know quietly to keep their hands visible too. An officer approaching a car can't immediately read everyone's intentions, and calm, visible hands from everyone in the vehicle help.

Your phone is worth a quick thought. If you were using it for navigation, it's probably already mounted and visible — that's fine. Don't pick it up during the stop. If you feel you need to record the encounter, know your local laws on that; rules vary, and trying to figure it out mid-stop isn't the moment.

Nerves are normal. Your voice might shake. That's human. Officers conduct stops constantly and can read the difference between someone who's anxious and someone who's being evasive. Being honest about nerves — "Sorry, I'm a little flustered" — is usually received fine.

If you believe the stop itself was improper, or if something happens that doesn't feel right, the roadside is not the place to argue it. Comply, stay safe, note the officer's name and badge number if you can, and address your concerns through the right channels afterward — which might mean filing a complaint or speaking with a lawyer.

After the stop

Once you're back on the road, give yourself a moment before you pull into traffic. Adrenaline takes a few minutes to clear, and that's not a great time to be making lane changes at speed.

If you had someone waiting to hear from you — a friend tracking your drive home, a family member who knew you'd be on the road — let them know you're okay. A quick update costs nothing and saves the people who care about you from worrying in silence.
