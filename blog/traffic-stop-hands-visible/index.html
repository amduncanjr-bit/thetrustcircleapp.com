Traffic stops: keeping your hands visible while your documents do the talking

A little preparation before you're ever pulled over can make the stop calmer for everyone in the car.

Most traffic stops last under ten minutes. They end without incident. But those few minutes can feel disorienting — flashing lights in your mirror, a stranger approaching your window, your heart doing something inconvenient. Having a clear mental script before it ever happens means you spend less of that time guessing what to do next.

Before the officer reaches your window

When you see the lights, signal and pull over as far right as you safely can. Turn off the engine. If it's dark, turn on your interior lights — this is a small gesture that communicates a lot. Put both hands on the top of the steering wheel and leave them there.

Don't reach for your registration or insurance card yet. Wait until the officer asks. Then, before you move, say out loud where you're going to reach: "My registration is in the glove box — I'm going to open it now." This isn't a legal requirement in most places, but it removes ambiguity. Movement without narration is what causes confusion. Narrated movement is just a person getting their paperwork.

If you have passengers, brief them the same way. Everyone stays calm, hands relaxed and visible, no sudden movement toward bags or compartments. Kids in the back seat should understand this too — not to frighten them, but because it's a practical life skill they'll carry for decades.

Confusion at a traffic stop usually comes from unexpected movement, not from anything you say.

Keep your license, registration, and proof of insurance somewhere you can reach them without digging. A small envelope clipped to your visor for the registration and insurance card means you're not rummaging through a glove box full of old receipts while an officer waits. That kind of preparation costs nothing and removes one source of stress entirely.

During the stop

Answer questions directly and honestly. You don't have to volunteer information beyond what's asked, but being cooperative tends to keep things short and civil. If you're unsure about your rights in a given situation — whether to consent to a search, for example — the honest answer is to know your local laws ahead of time, and to talk to a lawyer if you ever face a situation where it matters. This article isn't the place for legal advice, and a blog post shouldn't be your only source on that.

If you disagree with anything that happens during the stop, the roadside is rarely the right place to argue it. Document what you can remember afterward — time, location, officer's name and badge number if you have them, what was said. That record has more value than a confrontation that escalates a routine stop into something else.

If you feel genuinely unsafe at any point, you have options. You can ask if you're free to go. You can call 911 to report what's happening. In some situations, driving slowly to a well-lit public place before stopping — while putting on your hazards and, if possible, calling 911 to explain — may be appropriate. Know your local laws around this before you're ever in that position.

After the stop

Once you're back on the road, take a breath. If you pulled over somewhere uncomfortable — a dark stretch of highway, an unfamiliar area — text someone your location before you drive on. If you'd already set up a trip with someone watching, they've had your location the whole time; if not, a quick message takes thirty seconds and gives someone a thread to pull if anything ever went differently than expected.

The whole point of preparation is that you don't have to think under pressure. Hands visible, movements narrated, documents easy to find, someone who knows roughly where you are. That's it. None of it is complicated, and all of it helps.
